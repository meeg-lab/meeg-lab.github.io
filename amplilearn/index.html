AmpliLearn
amplicon workbench · 16S & ITS
runs entirely in your browser

A hands-on workbench for exploring amplicon microbiome data — OTU/ASV table + sample metadata in, diversity out. Rarefy your counts, then explore alpha and beta diversity, PCoA / NMDS ordinations, and taxonomic composition. Works the same for 16S or ITS. All computation happens locally in your browser via Python (Pyodide) — your data never leaves your machine.

STEP 1

Load your data

Two files. The OTU table is BIOM-style: OTUs/ASVs in the rows, samples in the columns, raw counts (not percentages), with an optional trailing taxonomy column. The metadata has one row per sample and columns of environmental variables (pH, habitat, etc.). Sample IDs must match between the two files.

What should my files look like? (read this before uploading)

AmpliLearn needs two separate files, and the sample IDs must match between them.

1 · OTU table (BIOM-style)

OTUs/ASVs in rows, samples in columns, raw whole-number counts. Optional last column named taxonomy.

#OTU IDSample_1Sample_2taxonomy
OTU00011520k__Bacteria; p__Firmicutes; …; g__Blautia
OTU0002841k__Bacteria; p__Bacteroidetes; …
2 · Sample metadata

One row per sample; first column = sample ID (must match the table's column names). Other columns are your variables.

sample_idgrouppH
Sample_1case6.2
Sample_2control7.1
Common mistakes that cause the “non-numeric values” error
  • Percentages / relative abundances instead of raw counts (values like 0.0195). AmpliLearn rarefies, which needs whole-number reads.
  • A group row hidden in the table — a #Group row of case/control. Group labels belong in the metadata file, not the OTU table.
  • Taxonomy used as the row name (e.g. Bacteria;Firmicutes;…) instead of an OTU ID plus a separate taxonomy column.
  • Sample IDs that don’t match between the two files — watch spaces, capitalisation, and - vs . vs _.

Exported from MicrobiomeAnalyst or mothur? Those tax-summary tables are relative abundances, not raw counts — use your raw OTU/ASV count table instead. The downloadable examples below show the exact layout.

STEP 2

Rarefaction

Samples are sequenced to different depths, and deeper samples look richer purely because more was sampled. Rarefaction subsamples every sample down to a common depth so diversity can be compared fairly. The curves below show how observed richness rises with depth — where a curve flattens, that sample is well-sampled. Pick a depth near the point where most curves plateau (samples below it are dropped).

STEP 3

Alpha diversity

Alpha diversity is diversity within a sample. Observed counts how many OTUs are present; Shannon and Simpson also weight by how even the abundances are; Pielou is evenness on its own. We compare groups with a Mann-Whitney (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis (more) test.

STEP 4

Beta diversity & ordination

Beta diversity is how different samples are from each other. We turn the table into a distance matrix (Bray-Curtis uses abundances, Jaccard presence/absence, Aitchison is compositional), then squeeze it into 2-D so you can see it: PCoA preserves distances directly, NMDS preserves their rank order (check its stress — under 0.2 is usable). Colour by a variable; for categories we run PERMANOVA to test if groups really differ.

STEP 5

Taxonomic composition

A stacked bar plot of who is there: each bar is a sample, each colour a taxon, and the height is its relative abundance. Choose a taxonomic rank and how many top taxa to show (the rest become “Other”). Sort the bars by an environmental variable to see the community turn over along a gradient. (Needs a taxonomy column.)
